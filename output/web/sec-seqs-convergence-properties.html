    Section 2.4 Convergence properties

    In this section we shall establish and discuss several properties of sequence convergence, i.e. common or general ways that convergent sequences behave, and how they interact with the operations of addition and multiplication that we can apply to sequences. Many of the ideas behind these properties will re-occur later in the context of the convergence of series, function values and properties of differentiation and integration.
    🔗
    The first result, Theorem 2.4.2, about the uniqueness of limits may seem obvious at first. But what if two different numbers were extremely close together on the number line - could a single sequence be getting closer and closer to both of them? The proof shows that this can not happen. It’s a good first example of how the formal definition can be used to prove things about convergence itself.
    🔗

    Note 2.4.1. Proof by contradiction.

    The proof is also a good demonstration of the logical principle of proof by contradiction. For the technique of proof-by-contradiction we make the assumption that the result we are trying to prove is in fact false. From this assumption we then derive a clear contradiction of something we already know to be true. The only way to resolve this then is to accept that the assumption we made is itself false, i.e that the result we are trying to prove is in fact true!
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.2. Uniqueness of limits.

    If a sequence converges then its limit is unique, i.e. if \(x_n \to L\) and \(x_n \to M\) as \(n \to \infty\text{,}\) then \(L=M\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Suppose that a sequence \(a_n\) converges to limits \(L , M \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) We use the technique of proof by contradiction. If we assume that \(L\) and \(M\) are distinct, i.e. that \(|L - M|>0\text{,}\) then we can let \(\epsilon = |L - M| / 2 > 0\text{.}\) Since \(a_n \to L\) there exists an \(N_1\) such that for every \(n \geq N_1\) we have \(|a_n - L| < \epsilon\text{.}\) Similarly, since \(a_n \to M\) there exists an \(N_2\) such that for every \(n \geq N_2\) we have \(|a_n - M| < \epsilon\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We wish to make use of both of these inequalities simultaneously. So we set \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\text{.}\) Now for every \(n \geq N\) we can say
    \begin{align*} |L - M|\amp = |L - a_n + a_n - M| \\ \amp \leq |L - a_n| + |a_n - M|, \text{ by the triangle inequality}\\ \amp \lt \epsilon + \epsilon \\ \amp = |L - M| / 2 + |L - M| / 2 \\ \amp = |L - M| \text{.} \end{align*}
    🔗
    This chain of relations has established that for all \(n \geq N\)
    \begin{equation*} |L - M| < |L - M| . \end{equation*}
    But this last statement is a clear contradiction as a real number cannot be strictly less than itself.
    🔗
    So we conclude that the assumption that led to this conclusion must itself be false, i.e. that \(L\) and \(M\) are indeed the same. This proves the result. ◻
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.4.3. Triangle inequality.

    The uniqueness proof used the triangle inequality The absolute value, \(|x|\text{,}\) of real numbers \(x\) satisfies the following property: for all real numbers \(x\) and \(y\) we have
    \begin{equation*} | x + y | \leq |x| + |y|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This can be shown by considering the four possibilities for the positivity/negativity of x and y. Generalizations of the triangle inequality hold in many other geometric and algebraic settings (e.g. vector spaces). The name triangle inequality comes from the context of vector geometry where this property expresses the fact that a side length of a triangle is always less than (or equal to in the case of a triangle degenerated to a straight line) the sum of the lengths of the other two sides.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next result, Theorem 2.4.4, says that every convergent sequence is bounded. Again this is something that seems quite plausible at first perhaps, but whose proof requires careful handling of the maximum principle.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.4. Convergent implies bounded.

    Every convergent sequence is bounded.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Consider a convergent sequence \(\{x_n\}\text{.}\) Suppose that \(x_n \to x\) as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\) We know there exists a positive integer \(N\) such that for all \(n \geq N\) we have \(|x_n - x| < 1\text{,}\) this implies that for all \(n \geq N\) we have \(|x_n| < 1 + |x|\text{.}\) This shows that the sequence is eventually bounded, i.e. after element \(N\text{.}\) But this just leaves a finite number of terms at the beginning to consider. So let \(M = \max(|x_1|, |x_2|, \dots , |x_{N-1}|, 1+|x|)\text{.}\) Then we can say that for all \(n \geq 1\) we have \(|x_n| \leq M\text{,}\) i.e. that the sequence is bounded.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.4.5. Maximums (or minimums) of infinite sets.

    Could we not have proved this theorem in one line? By setting the bound \(\) to be the maximum absolute value of the sequence elements? Well actually it is not that simple. The maximum (or minimum) of a finite set of numbers is unproblematic - it is simply the largest (or smallest) of the numbers in the set. However care must be taken when considering the maximum (or minimum) of an infinite set, as the max or min may not exist. Consider the sequence \(\left \{ a_n \right \}_{n=1}^\infty\) where \(a_n = 2^n\text{.}\) This sequence has no maximum value!
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.4.6. Why \(\epsilon =1?\)

    There is nothing special about the use of \(\epsilon = 1\) in this proof that a convergent sequence is bounded. We could have used any strictly positive value. All that was needed was to separate the sequence elements into two sets, a finite set of sequence elements at the beginning of the sequence (that we can then take the maximum absolute value of), and then the infinite tail of the sequence that is bounded within the interval of radius \(\epsilon\) around the limit \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Knowing that a sequence is bounded and either increasing or decreasing is enough to guarantee that it converges.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.7. Monotone Convergence Theorem.

    Every bounded monotonic sequence is convergent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We shall not discuss the proof of this is in detail in this course. It essentially follows from the least-upper bound principle of the real numbers.
    🔗
    🔗
    The next theorem describes how convergence interacts with the arithmetic operations of addition and multiplication that we can extend to sequences.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.8. Algebra of limits theorem.

    Let \(\left \{ a_n \right \}\) and \(\left \{ b_n \right \}\) be two convergent sequences with
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = a \text{ and } \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = b \text{,} \end{equation*}
    where \(a,b \in \R\) and \(a,b \neq 0\text{.}\) New sequences can be formed from the elements \(a_n\) and \(b_n\) using addition and multiplication. The following new sequences are all convergent and have the expected limits:
    🔗
    • Linear Combinations:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \left ( \alpha a_n + \beta b_n \right )= \alpha a + \beta b, \end{equation*}
      where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}, \, \alpha,\beta \neq 0\) are any pair of non-zero coefficients,
      🔗
      🔗
    • Products:
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \left ( a_n b_n \right ) = ab, \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • Quotients: (assuming that \(b \neq 0\) and for all \(n\text{,}\) \(b_n \neq 0\))
      \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \left ( \frac{a_n}{b_n} \right ) = \frac{a}{b}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    First we prove the linear combinations part.
    🔗
    Assume that we have convergent sequences \(\{a_n\}\) and \(\{b_n\}\) with \(a_n \to a\) and \(b_n \to b\text{.}\) Let \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\text{.}\) Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be given. Since both sequences are convergent we can say that there exists \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) such that for all \(n \geq N_1\) we have \(|a_n - a| < \frac{\epsilon}{2 |\alpha|}\text{,}\) and that for all \(n \geq N_2\) we have \(| b_n - b| < \frac{\epsilon}{2 |\beta|}\text{.}\) We wish to make use of both inequalities simultaneously so we let \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\text{.}\) Then for all \(n \geq N\) we have
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} | (\alpha a_n + \beta b_n) - (\alpha a + \beta b) | & = | \alpha (a_n - a) + \beta (b_n - b) | \\ &\leq | \alpha| |a_n - a| + |\beta | |b_n - b |, \\ \hfill & \text{ triangle inequality and factorising}\\ &< |\alpha| \frac{\epsilon}{2 |\alpha|} + |\beta | \frac{\epsilon}{2 |\beta|}, \text{ from above}\\ & = \epsilon . \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    From the above relations we can extract the required criterion for convergence, namely for all \(n \geq N\) we have \(| (\alpha a_n + \beta b_n) - (\alpha a + \beta b) | < \epsilon\text{.}\) This proves the linearity part of the theorem.
    🔗
    Next we prove the products part.
    🔗
    Suppose that \(a_n \to a\) and \(b_n \to b\) as before and that \(\epsilon >0\) is given.
    🔗
    By convergence there exist positive integers \(N_1\) and \(N_1\) such that for all \(n \geq N_1\) we have \(|a_n - a| < \min\left ( \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}, \frac{\epsilon}{3|b|} \right )\) and for all \(n \geq N_2\) we have \(|b_n - b| < \min\left ( \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}, \frac{\epsilon}{3|a|} \right )\text{.}\) We wish to use both ineqaualties so we let \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\) and now for all \(n \geq N\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} |a_n b_n - ab | & = | (a_n - a)(b_n - b) + b(a_n - a) + a(b_n - b)|\\ &\leq | (a_n - a)(b_n - b)| + |b||(a_n - a)| + |a||(b_n - b)|\\ &< \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{3}}+ |b|\frac{\epsilon}{3|b|} + |a|\frac{\epsilon}{3|a|}\\ &= \epsilon \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    From the above relations we can extract the required criterion for convergence, namely for all \(n \geq N\) we have \(| a_n b_n - a b | < \epsilon\text{.}\) This proves the product part of the theorem.
    🔗
    Next we prove the quotients part. We will simplify matters by first proving a special case that if \(b_n \to b\) (with \(b_n, b \neq 0\)) then \(1/b_n \to 1/b\text{.}\) Then the general quotient case can be derived from this special case and the product case, since we can re-express \(a_n / b_n\) as \(a_n (1/b_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The relevant absolute value to examine is
    \begin{equation*} \left | \frac{1}{b_n} - \frac{1}{b} \right | = \left | \frac{b - b_n}{b_n b} \right | = \frac{1}{|b||b_n|} \left | b_n - b \right |. \end{equation*}
    To ensure that this absolute value is less than \(\epsilon\) we will have to arrange a lower bound for the denominator and a suitable upper bound for the numerator. By convergence there exists a positive integer \(N_1\) such that for all \(n \geq N_1\) we have \(|b_n - b| < |b|/2\text{,}\) which implies that \(|b_n| > |b|/2\text{.}\) Also by convergence, there exists a positive integer \(N_2\) such that for all \(n \geq N_2\) we have \(|b_n - b| < \epsilon |b|^2 / 2\text{.}\) So letting \(N = \max(N_1, N_2)\) then for all \(n \geq N\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \left | \frac{1}{b_n} - \frac{1}{b} \right |= \frac{1}{|b||b_n|} \left | b_n - b \right | < \frac{2}{|b||b|} \frac{|b|^2}{2} \epsilon = \epsilon, \end{equation*}
    which gives us the required inequality to conclude that \(1/b_n \to 1/b\) as claimed. Together with the remarks above this proves the quotients part of the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Note 2.4.9.

    The proof of the algebra of limits theorem exploits the formal definition of convergence in various ways depending on the context. In each case, what guides the particular choice of \(\epsilon\) value for the known convergence of \(a_n\) or \(b_n\) is the desire to achieve the appropriate inequality in the end of the form
    \begin{equation*} \left | \text{sequence element} - \text{limit} \right | \lt \epsilon \text{.} \end{equation*}
    Study the three cases carefully and work to an understanding of why the various values of \(\epsilon\) were chosen.
    🔗
    🔗
    The algebra of limits theorem can allow us to find the limits of sequences that are formed from sequences with known convergence properties. By referring to the theorem we can provide rigorous arguments to justify the limit of the sequence in question, based on limits of simpler sequences.
    🔗

    Example 2.4.10. Applying the algebra of limits theorem.

    Consider the sequence \(\{ x_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) where
    \begin{equation*} x_n = \frac{2}{3}\frac{2n^2 -n}{\left ( -3n^2 +2 \right )}. \end{equation*}
    By applying the various parts of the algebra of limits theorem we can see how to find the limit of this convergent sequence.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \lim_{n \to \infty} x_n &= \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2}{3}\frac{2n^2 -n}{\left ( -3n^2 +2 \right )} \\ &= \frac{2}{3} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{2 - \frac{1}{n}}{\left ( -3 + \frac{2}{n^2} \right )}, \quad \text{linearity} \\ &= \frac{2}{3} \frac{\lim\limits_{n \to \infty} \left (2 - \frac{1}{n} \right )}{\lim\limits_{n \to \infty} \left ( -3 + \frac{2}{n^2} \right )}, \quad \text{quotient rule}\\ &= \frac{2}{3} \left ( \frac{2}{-3} \right ), \quad \text{linearity and known convergence of $\frac{1}{n}$ and $\frac{1}{n^2}$} \\ & = \frac{-4}{9} \end{aligned}\text{.} \end{equation*}
