    Section 6.3 The definite integral

    Subsection 6.3.1 A motivating example

    Consider an object travelling with constant velocity \(u\text{,}\) starting at \(t_1\text{,}\) and ending at\(t_2\text{.}\) Then the displacement, \(s\text{,}\) (the total distance travelled) of the object will be given by
    \begin{equation*} s = u(t_2 - t_1). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Key idea: products like this can often be conceived geometrically as areas as in Figure 6.3.1. The total displacement can be thought of as the accumulated velocity over the time period, or the continuos summation of all the velocity over the time period.
    🔗
    Then we can extend this concept to situations with changing velocity. The displacement will still correspond to the accumulated area, i.e. the area between the velocity curve \(u(t)\) and the \(t\) axis. However regions where the velocity becomes negative, i.e. turns in the opposite direction, will have to be counted as a negative contribution to the area/displacement. This is depicted in figure FIGURE where the positive areas are shaded yellow and the ngative areas shaded red.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 6.3.2 Definite integral and Riemann integration

    Definition 6.3.3.

    Let \(a \lt b\text{.}\) For a continuous function \(f(x)\) defined on a closed interval \([a,b]\) of the real numbers, the definite integral of \(f\) over the interval \([a,b]\) is defined to be the total signed area lying between the graph of the function \(f\) and the \(x\)-axis. That is, regions where \(f(x)\) is positive will contribute positively to the toal area, and regions where \(f(x)\) is negative will contribute negatively to the total area. The following notation is used for this definite integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b \, f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We are now faced with a general problem. Given a variable function \(f(x)\text{,}\) how do we actually compute the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x) \, dx\text{?}\) For certain nice functions we might be able to use known geometric results for the areas of rectangles, triangles, circles etc, to evaluate such integrals. But that does not cover most functions. For instance, what is the definite integral of the function \(x^2\) for instance? Thankfully we have a systematic way of defining the definite integral via a limit of a sequence of approximations to the total signed area underneath the graph. This method is known as the Riemann integral.
    🔗

    Key idea:.

    The key idea behind Riemann inegration is to construct an approximation for the definite integral \(\int_a^b f(x) \, dx\) consisting of a collection of rectangles that span the interval \([a,b]\) and whose heights come up to meet the graph of the function. See Figure 6.3.4 for a sketch of such a situation.
    🔗
    In the figure we can see how the sum of the areas of the rectangles is an approximation to the area underneath the curve, i.e. to the definite integral. However it is not exact as due to the fluctuating value of the function \(f(x)\) there are places where the rectangles poke out above the function (shaded green) and places where the rectangles fail to cover all the space beneath the curve (shaded red). However if we allowed outselves to use more and thinnner rectangles, then these over and under estimations would be reduced. And perhaps if we took the limit as the number of rectangles tended to \(\infty\) and their widths tended to \(0\) then, in the limit, we may capture the value of the definite integral exactly.
    🔗
    This is the idea behind Riemann integration. With the following definitions we set up the necessary notation and terminology to be able to formally define the Riemann integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 6.3.5.

    Let \(a \lt b\text{.}\) A partition of an interval \([a,b]\) is a finite sequence of numbers \(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots , x_n\text{,}\) with \(x_0 = a, x_n = b\) and for all \(0 \leq i \lt n\text{,}\) \(x_i \lt x_{i+1}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    So a partition divides the interval up into \(n\) sub-intervals \([x_i, x_{i+1}]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 6.3.6. Riemann integral.

    • Let \(a \lt b\text{,}\) and let \(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots , x_n\) be a partition of the interval \([a,b]\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Let \(\Delta x_{i-1} = x_i - x_{i-1}\) denote the length of the \(i^{th}\) sub-interval.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Let \(x_i^*\) denote a representative point from the \(i^{th}\) sub-interval, i.e. \(x_{i-1} \leq x_i^* \leq x_i\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Let \(\Delta x = \max \left ( \{ \Delta x_{i-1} \, \mid \, i=1, \dots , n \} \right )\) be the maximum width of the sub-intervals.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The Riemann integral definition of the definite integral is then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \lim\limits_{\substack{n \to \infty \\ \Delta x \to 0}} \left ( \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*) \Delta x_{i-1} \right ). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It pays to take the time to study this definition and appreciate how it is the limit of the sum of the areas of rectangles underneath the curve, the limit being taken as both the number of rectangles tends to infinity and the maximum width of those rectangles tends to zero.
    🔗
    This is a very important definition and will allow use both
    • compute the definite integrals of various functions,
      🔗
      🔗
    • prove results about the definite integral concept itself, such as the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    When computing Riemann integrals of polynomials we will often need to make use of various summation formulae for consecutive powers of integers, such as
    • \(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^n j = \frac{n(n+1)}{2},\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^n j^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^n j^3 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}.\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
