    Section 2.2 Convergence

    Definition 2.2.1. Convergence for series.

    The series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n\) converges to the limit (or sum) \(L\) if the sequence of its partial sums, \(\{ S_k \}_{k=1}^\infty\text{,}\) is a convergent sequence and
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{k \to \infty} S_k = L . \end{equation*}
    If this is the case then we can simply write
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty x_n = L . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    A series is divergent if the sequence of its partial sums is a divergent sequence.
    🔗
    🔗
    It is not hard to think of examples of divergent series.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.2. A divergent series.

    Consider the series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty m . \end{equation*}
    This series is clearly divergent as the partial sums are increasing without any upper bound, and so cannot form a convergent sequence (see theorem Theorem 1.4.4).
    🔗
    🔗
    However it is not so obvious that convergent series exist (beyond the trivial example of a series where the terms are all zero after some point).
    🔗

    Example 2.2.3. A convergent series.

    Consider the series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \left ( \frac{1}{2} \right )^m . \end{equation*}
    The convergence of this series can be appreciated when we interpret the terms as measuring the size of step taken along a line. We see that the partial sums of the series get closer and closer to 1, but never go beyond 1. Indeed the series does converge to 1. This can be proved using the partial sum formula for a geometric series and making use of the algebra of sequences theorem.
    🔗
    🔗
    This example shows that it is possible to have an infinite number of terms and yet for them still to sum to a finite value. This happens because the terms are getting smaller and smaller, the terms themselves are converging to zero, when viewed as sequence. However this type of behaviour does not guarantee convergence for a series, as the next example shows.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.4. The harmonic series.

    Consider the series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{m}. \end{equation*}
    The terms of this series are indeed converging to zero, but yet the series does not converge. It increases without any upper bound, albeit very slowly. This fact can be proved with the aid of a grouping argument.
    🔗
    🔗
    For series that contain a mixture of positive and negative terms it is helpful to be able to refer to the convergence of the related series of absolute values.
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.5. Absolute convergence.

    A series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is absolutely convergent if the series of absolute values, \(\sum _{m=1}^\infty \left | a_m \right |\text{,}\) is a convergent series. If the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is convergent but the series of absolute values, \(\sum _{m=1}^\infty \left | a_m \right |\text{,}\) is divergent, then the original series is said to be conditionally convergent.
    🔗
    🔗
    The importance of conditional convergence is given by the following result which we shall quote without proof.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.6.

    If a series is absolutely convergent then it is convergent. Moreover, the terms of an absolutely convergent series can be rearranged in any way and the resulting series will still converge to the same sum. However if a series is conditionally convergent then rearranging its terms can yield a series which sums to a different value.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Omitted.
    🔗
    🔗

    Tails.

    For an infinite series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + a_4 + \dots \end{equation*}
    we note that the convergence or divergence of the series is not altered if some finite collection of the terms of the series are changed.
    🔗
    For suppose that we change \(a_1\) to \(a_1 + \delta_1\) and \(a_2\) to \(a_2 + \delta_2\text{.}\) If the original series is convergent, say \(\sum a_m = A\) then changing these values will mean the new series converges to \(A+\delta_1+\delta_2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    On the other hand if the series is divergent it is because the sequence of partial sums, \(S_k = \sum_{m=1}^k a_m\text{,}\) fails to converge. The partial sums, \(S_k'\text{,}\) of the new series, for \(k \geq 3\text{,}\) will satisfy \(S_k' = S_k + \delta_1+\delta_2\text{,}\) and so these too will still fail to converge.
    🔗
    Both of these arguments extend to any finite collection of terms. By a tail of the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) we mean any series of the form \(\sum_{m=M}^\infty a_m\text{,}\) for some positive integer \(M\text{,}\) i.e. the original series less some finite collection of initial terms. If we can prove that some tail of the series is convergent, then so will the original series be convergent. Similarly, if we can show that some tail of the series is divergent, then so will the original series be divergent. These ideas occur in some of the proofs of the series convergence tests later in the chapter.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.7. Linear combinations of series.

    Suppose that we have two convergent series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) and \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty b_m\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m = A , \text{ and } \sum_{m=1}^\infty b_m = B . \end{equation*}
    Then for any pair \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\) we have a new series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \left ( \alpha a_m + \beta b_m \right ) , \end{equation*}
    which is convergent and satisfies
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \left ( \alpha a_m + \beta b_m \right ) = \alpha A + \beta B. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This can be proved using the linearity property for convergence of sequences. We do not need to descend to \(\epsilon, N\) arguments but just use the definition for series convergence in terms of the partial sums.
    🔗
    Letting \(A_k\) and \(B_k\) denote the partial sums, i.e. \(A_k = \sum_{n=1}^k a_n\) and \(B_k = \sum_{n=1}^k b_n\) we make the following deductions.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \alpha A + \beta B & = \alpha \lim_{k\to \infty}A_k + \beta \lim_{k\to \infty}B_k\\ &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left ( \alpha A_k + \beta B_k \right ), \text{ by linearity of limits for sequences}\\ &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left ( \alpha \sum_{n=1}^k a_n + \beta \sum_{n=1}^k b_n \right )\\ &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^k \left ( \alpha a_n + \beta b_n \right ), \text{ manipulation of sum}\\ &=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left ( \alpha a_n + \beta b_n \right ), \text{ by def. of series convergence}. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Question 2.2.8.

    Why was the presentation reversed here? In that we started with the claimed limit \(\alpha A + \beta B\) and worked our way towards \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left ( \alpha a_n + \beta b_n \right )\text{?}\)
