    Section 2.4 Power Series

    Using series we can define a class of functions called power series. We shall look at some basic examples here and return to this topic later when we are discussing the differential calculus.
    🔗

    Definition 2.4.1. Power Series.

    A sequence of terms \(\{ a_m \}_{m=1}^\infty\) defines a power series function \(A\) by the formula
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m x^m . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Of course this formula for \(A(x)\) may only make sense for certain values of the argument \(x\text{.}\) For some values of \(x\) the series may converge but for some values it may diverge. We can investigate this matter using the ratio test.
    🔗
    According to the ratio test the series formula for \(A(x)\) will converge when
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \left | \frac{a_{m+1} x^{m+1}}{a_m x^m} \right | < 1, \end{equation*}
    which is equivalent, after simplifying, to
    \begin{equation*} |x| < \lim_{m \to \infty} \left | \frac{a_m}{a_{m+1}} \right |. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 2.4.2. Radius of Convergence.

    The quantity \(R\) defined by
    \begin{equation*} R = \lim_{m \to \infty} \left | \frac{a_m}{a_{m+1}} \right | , \end{equation*}
    is known as the radius of convergence of the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m x^m\text{.}\) The series converges if \(|x| < R\) and diverges if \(|x| > R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Note that the ratio test does not tell us about the convergence of the series when \(|x| =R\text{.}\) That will have to be determined by other methods.
    🔗
    An important source of power series comes from applying Taylor’s theorem.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.3. Taylor’s Theorem.

    Suppose that on an interval \([a,x]\) the function \(f\) can be differentiated \(n+1\) times, for some \(n\geq 0\text{.}\) Then there exists a number \(c \in (a,x)\) such that
    \begin{equation*} f(x)=f(a) + f'(a) (x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!} (x-a)^2 + \dots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!} (x-a)^n + R_n , \end{equation*}
    where \(R_n\) is the Lagrange remainder and is given by
    \begin{equation*} R_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!} (x-a)^{n+1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Proof omitted.
    🔗
    🔗
    Taylor’s theorem provides us with a way of generating power series approximations to function values. By writing \(a+x\) for \(x\) in the above equation we get an equivalent form referred to as the Taylor polynomial expansion of \(f\) about the point \(a\):
    \begin{equation*} f(a+x)=f(a) + f'(a) x + \frac{f''(a)}{2!} x^2 + \dots + \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!} x^n + R_n . \end{equation*}
    It is often the case that \(R_n(x) \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\) and then we may represent the function \(f\) using its Taylor series expansion about the point \(a\):
    \begin{equation*} f(a+x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)} (a)}{n!} x^n \, . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Two important things to note are that for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{x^n}{n!} = 0 \end{equation*}
    and that if \(-1<x<1\) then
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \to \infty} x^n = 0 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The special case where we expand a function about the point \(a=0\) gives rise to what is called a Maclaurin series.
    🔗

    Definition 2.4.4. Maclaurin series.

    The Taylor series expansion of a function \(f\) about the point \(0\) is called the Maclaurin series of \(f\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} x^n . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    We shall return to the topic of Taylor series after we have studied differentiation in more detail.
