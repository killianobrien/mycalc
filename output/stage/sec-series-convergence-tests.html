    Section 2.3 Convergence tests

    We will introduce various tests that can be used to assess the convergence / divergence of a series.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.1. General Term Test.

    If a sequence has a non-zero limit (or no limit at all) then the associated series will diverge. Equivalently, by forming the contra-positive we can quote this test as: If a series converges then the associated sequence of terms converges to zero. Symbolically these two statements are
    • \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} a_m \neq 0 \, \Rightarrow \, \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m \text{ diverges,} \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    • \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m \text{ converges} \, \Rightarrow \, \lim_{m \to \infty} a_m = 0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Suppose that \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n\) converges and let \(A_k\) denote the partial sum as before, i.e. \(A_k \to a\) as \(k \to \infty\text{.}\) Let \(\epsilon >0\) be given. There exists a \(K\) such that for all \(k \geq K\) we have \(\left | A_k - a \right | < \epsilon / 2\text{.}\) This implies that for all \(k \geq K\) we have \(|A_k - A_{k+1} | = |a_{k+1}| < \epsilon\text{,}\) i.e. the sequence can be shown to be eventually arbitrarily close to zero. This is equivalent to the definition for \(a_n \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\text{.}\) So this proves the second form of the theorem.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.2. Contrapositive.

    We note the use of the contrapositive in the statement of the General Term Test. The contrapositive of a statement \(A \Rightarrow B\) is the statement \(\neg B \Rightarrow \neg A\text{.}\) These two statements are logically equivalent, i.e. they both have the same truth value. If one is true then so is the other. If the result one is trying to prove take the form of an implication, then sometimes the contrapositive of the result may be easier to prove directly.
    🔗
    🔗
    But note that a series can still diverge even if the sequence of terms converges to zero. For example the harmonic series,
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{m} , \end{equation*}
    diverges even though \(\frac{1}{m} \to 0\) as \(m \to \infty\text{.}\) The General Term Test gives no information about the behaviour of the harmonic series. The divergence of the harmonic series can be shown by using the integral test (see later) as well as the earlier term-grouping argument.
    🔗
    A geometric series is, as the name suggests, the sum of the terms of a geometric sequence. The convergence of a geometric series can be determined using the following test.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.3. Geometric Series Test.

    The geometric series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a r^{n-1}\) converges trivially if \(a=0\text{.}\) If \(a \neq 0\) then it converges if and only if \(|r| \lt 1\text{,}\) in which case
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty a r^{n-1} = \frac{a}{1-r} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The convergent case \(a=0\) is clear. So we assume that \(a \neq 0\text{.}\) If \(r=1\) then divergence is clear as the series is just \(a+a+a+a+ \dots\text{.}\) So assume \(r \neq 1\) and let \(A_k\) denote the partial sum, i.e. \(A_k = \sum_{n=1}^k a_n\text{.}\) Most of the terms in the difference \(A_k - rA_k\) will cancel leaving the closed form equation
    \begin{equation*} A_k = \frac{a(1 - r^k)}{1-r}. \end{equation*}
    Then by the definition of convergence foran infinite series and the algebra of limits theorem we have
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{n=1}^\infty a r^{n-1} = \lim_{k \to \infty} A_k = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{a(1 - r^k)}{1-r} = \frac{a(1 - \lim_{k \to \infty} r^k)}{1-r}. \end{equation*}
    If \(r = -1\) or \(|r| > 1\) then \(r^k\) diverges, and hence so does the series. If \(|r| < 1\) then \(r^k \to 0\) as \(k \to \infty\text{,}\) in which case the sum of the series is \(a/(1-r)\text{,}\) as required.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.5. Integral Test.

    Consider a series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) and suppose that there exists a continuous function \(f\) which has positive decreasing values and that \(a_m = f(m)\) for all \(m \geq 1\text{.}\) If the improper integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^\infty f(t) \, dt = \lim_{T \to \infty} \, \int_1^T f(t) \, dt \end{equation*}
    exists and is finite, then the original series converges. If the improper integral above does not have a finite value then the original series diverges.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We sketch the main idea of the proof here based on the typical situation depicted in figure Figure 2.3.4. To make the argument rigourous we should rephrase it in terms of the partial sums and partial integrals and use a careful limiting argument.
    🔗
    There we see the graph of decreasing function \(f\) shown in black. The grey rectangles all have width 1 and the \(i^{th}\) rectangle has height \(a_i = f(i)\text{.}\) Interpreting the integral and series in terms of area we see that \(\int_1^\infty \, f(t) \, dt\) is the area between the graph and the \(x\)-axis from \(1\) to \(\infty\) and that the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is the total area of all the rectangles. Due to the decreasing nature of the function \(f\text{,}\) each rectangle protrudes a bit above the graph of \(f\text{.}\) If we let \(\gamma\) denote the sum of the areas of all these protrusions then we have
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m = \int_1^\infty \, f(t) \, dt + \gamma. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(\gamma\) is a well defined finite quantity as the sum of the areas of the protrusions constitute an increasing sequence of partial sums that is bounded above (for they would all fit inside the first rectangle without overlaps) and hence by the Monotone Convergence Theorem this series is convergent.
    🔗
    So if the original series and integral differ by a finite quantity \(\gamma\) then each converges if and only if the other does.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.6. Euler-Mascheroni constant.

    In the case where the function \(f\) is the reciprocal function \(f(t) = 1/t\text{,}\) the quantity \(\gamma\) is known as the Euler-Mascheroni constant, an important quantity in analysis and number theory where it occurs in many settings. It is still not known whether the Euler-Mscheroni constant \(\gamma\) is irrational or not, though most would bet that it is.
    🔗
    🔗
    There now follow two types of comparison test which assess the convergence of a given series by comparing its terms to the terms of a known convergent or divergent series.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.7. Standard Comparison Test.

    Suppose that \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is a series consisting of non-negative terms, i.e. for all \(m\text{,}\) \(a_m \geq 0\text{,}\) and that \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty c_m\) is a known convergent series of non-negative terms and \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty d_m\) is a known divergent series of non-negative terms. Then
    • if for all \(m \geq 1\text{,}\) \(a_m \leq c_m\text{,}\) then \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is convergent,
      🔗
      🔗
    • if for all \(m \geq 1\text{,}\) \(a_m \geq d_m\text{,}\) then \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is divergent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We sketch the main idea of the proofs. Let \(c\) denote the sum of the convergent comparator series, i.e. \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty c_m = c\text{.}\) Then the partial sums \(\sum_{m=1}^k c_m\) of the series constitute an increasing sequence converging to \(c\text{.}\) Suppose that for all \(m\geq 1\) we have \(a_m \leq c_m\text{.}\) Since each partial sum \(\sum_{m=1}^k a_m\) is bounded above by \(c\) (as \(c\) is an upper-bound for the partial sum \(\sum_{m=1}^k c_m\)), these partial sums consitute an increasing sequence which is bounded above. Therefore, by the Monotone Convergence theorem Theorem 1.4.7, the sequence of partial sums \(\sum_{m=1}^k a_m\) is convergent as \(k \to \infty\text{,}\) and converges to a limit \(a\) which also satisfies \(a \leq c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The partial sums, \(\sum_{m=1}^k d_m\text{,}\) of a divergent series of positive terms will constitute an unbounded increasing sequence. Suppose that for all \(m \geq 1\) we have \(a_m \geq d_m\text{.}\) Then the partial sums satisfy
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^k a_m \geq \sum_{m=1}^k d_m, \end{equation*}
    and hence the partial sums \(\sum_{m=1}^k a_m\) also form an increasing unbounded sequence, hence divergent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.8. Good and bad behaviour.

    A nice way to think about/remember this result is in terms of the good and bad behaviour shown by a series. A convergent series \(\sum c_m\) of positive terms converges because the terms \(c_m\) behave well (i.e. are small enough). If \(a_m \leq c_m\) then the terms \(a_m\) are behaving better and so \(\sum a_m\) also converges.
    🔗
    Similarly a divergent series \(\sum d_m\) diverges because its terms behave badly (i.e. they’re too large), so if \(a_m \geq d_m\) then the terms \(a_m\) are behaving worse, and so \(\sum a_m\) also diverges.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.9. Limit Comparison Test.

    Suppose that \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is a series consisting of non-negative terms, i.e. for all \(m\text{,}\) \(a_m \geq 0\text{,}\) and that \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty c_m\) is a known convergent series of strictly positive terms (i.e. for all \(m\text{,}\) \(c_m > 0\)) and \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty d_m\) is a known divergent series of strictly positive terms. Then
    • if \(\lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_m}{c_m} = L\) and \(0 \leq L < \infty\) then \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is convergent,
      🔗
      🔗
    • if \(\lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_m}{d_m} = L\) and \(0 < L \leq \infty\) , then \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) is divergent.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    This limit comparison test can be proved with the aid of the standard comparison test applied to a suitable tail of the series. First we prove the convergent case. By the convergence of the ratio \(a_m/c_m\) there exists a positive integer \(M\) such that for all \(m \geq M\) we have \(|a_m/c_m - L|<1\text{,}\) hence \(a_m/c_m < L+1\) and hence \(a_m < (L+1)c_m\text{.}\) Therefore the standard comparison test says that the tail series \(\sum_{m=M}^\infty a_m\) converges and hence so does the original series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The divergent case is handled in a similar manner. By the convergence of the ratio \(a_m/d_m\) there exists a positive integer \(M\) such that for all \(m \geq M\) we have \(|a_m/d_m - L| < L/2\text{,}\) hence \(a_m/d_m > L/2\) and hence \(a_m > (L/2)d_m\text{.}\) Therefore the standard comparison test says that the tail series \(\sum_{m=M}^\infty a_m\) diverges and hence so does the original series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.3.10. More good and bad behaviour.

    Again this result can be boiled down to good/bad behaviour. If \(\lim_{m \to \infty} a_m/x_m = L\) then eventually \(a_m\) acts like a scalar multiple of \(x_m\text{.}\) So if \(\sum x_m\) behaves well (or badly), so does \(\sum a_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The choice for the comparison series is often a geometric series or a hyper-harmonic series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{1}{m^p} \end{equation*}
    for some \(p\text{.}\) Using the integral test we can show that the hypermonic series converges when \(p>1\) and diverges otherwise. The alternating hyper-harmonic series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n}{m^p} \end{equation*}
    converges absolutely when \(p > 1\text{,}\) converges conditionally when \(0 < p \leq 1\) and diverges otherwise.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.12. Alternating Series Test.

    Consider the alternating series
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{m=1}^\infty (-1)^m a_m , \end{equation*}
    where the \(a_m\) are all positive and decreasing, i.e. for all \(m\geq 1\text{,}\) \(a_{m} \geq a_{m+1} \geq 0\text{.}\) If \(\lim_{m \to \infty} a_m = 0\) then the original alternating series is convergent. But if \(\lim_{m \to \infty} a_m \neq 0\) then the alternating series is divergent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We give a sketch of the main idea here. Consider a typical alternating series satisfying the conditions of the theorem. The partial sums, \(A_k\text{,}\) of such a series are shown plotted in figure Figure 2.3.11
    🔗
    The partial sums are split into two subsequences, those of odd index, \(A_1, A_3, A_5, \dots\text{,}\) shown in grey, and those of even index, \(A_2, A_4, A_6, \dots\text{,}\) shown in black.
    🔗
    Due to the alternating nature of the series and the decreasing absolute value of the step size, i.e. the difference between each partial sum and the next, the odd subsequence of partial sums constitutes an increasing sequence which is bounded above. Similarly the even subsequence constitutes a decreasing sequence which is bounded below. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem each subsequence is convergent, i.e. \(A_{2k} \to L_{\text{even}}\) and \(A_{2k + 1} \to L_{\text{odd}}\) as \(k \to \infty\text{.}\) But since the step size, \(|a_m|\text{,}\) converges to zero, the difference between \(L_{\text{even}}\) and \(L_{\text{odd}}\) must be arbitrarily small, i.e. zero and hence \(L_{\text{even}} = L_{\text{odd}}\) and the complete sequence of partial sums is convergent to this limit, i.e. the series is convergent.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.13. Ratio Test.

    Consider the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) of positive terms, i.e. for all \(m\text{,}\) \(a_m > 0\text{.}\) Suppose that the sequence of the ratios of consectutive terms is convergent and that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_{m+1}}{a_m} =L . \end{equation*}
    If \(0 \leq L < 1\) then the series is convergent. If \(L>1\) then the series is divergent. For the remaining case \(L=1\) we can not draw a conclusion about the series’ convergence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The proof of the ratio test involves a combination of a limiting argument, the application of the standard comparison test and the use of the geometric series test. It’s a nice example of building upon previous results in order to establish new results.
    🔗
    Suppose \(\sum a_m\) satisfies the conditions of the theorem and that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_{m+1}}{a_m} =L < 1. \end{equation*}
    Choose a suitable \(\epsilon\text{,}\) say \(\epsilon = (1-L)/2\text{.}\) Then there exists a positive integer \(M\) such that for all \(m \geq M\) we have \(|a_{m+1}/a_m - L|< (1-L)/2\text{,}\) i.e. \(a_{m+1}/a_m < (L+1)/2 < 1\text{.}\) Hence, for all \(m \geq M\) we have
    \begin{equation*} a_{m+1} < \frac{L+1}{2} a_m. \end{equation*}
    Repeatedly applying this inequality allows us to say that for all \(n \geq 1\) we have
    \begin{equation*} a_{M+n} < \left ( \frac{L+1}{2} \right )^n a_M. \end{equation*}
    Note that the series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left ( \frac{L+1}{2} \right )^n a_M\) is a convergent geometric series, since \(\frac{L+1}{2} < 1\text{.}\) So by the standard comparison test the series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_{M+n}\) is also convergent. Therefore our original series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) has a convergent tail, and hence is convergent as required.
    🔗
    On the other hand is we suppose that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_{m+1}}{a_m} =L > 1, \end{equation*}
    then we follow a similar argument that this time compares a tail of our series to a divergent geometric series \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left ( \frac{L+1}{2} \right )^n a_M\text{,}\) for some \(M\) (remember this time \((L+1)/2 > 1\)). This implies that our series has a divergent tail and hence is itself divergent, as required.
    🔗
    In the remaining case where
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \frac{a_{m+1}}{a_m} =1, \end{equation*}
    then trying the above ideas will only allow us to say that \(\sum a_m\) has worse behaviour than a known convergent series and better behaviour than a known divergent series. But neither of these allow us to draw a conclusion about the behaviour of \(\sum a_m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 2.3.14. Root Test.

    Consider the series \(\sum_{m=1}^\infty a_m\) of non-negative terms, i.e. for all \(m\text{,}\) \(a_m \geq 0\text{.}\) Suppose that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{m \to \infty} \left ( a_m \right )^{\frac{1}{m}} = L . \end{equation*}
    If \(0 \leq L < 1\) then the original series is convergent, if \(1 < L \leq \infty\) then the series diverges. For the remaining case \(L=1\) we can not draw a conclusion about the series’ convergence.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We can prove this by comparing \(\sum a_m\) to a geometric series with common ratio \(L\text{.}\) Hence the re-appearance of the \(L<1\) and \(L>1\) criteria. The details are left as an exercise.
